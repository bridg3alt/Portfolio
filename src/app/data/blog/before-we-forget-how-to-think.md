*A Reflection on AI Safety, Human Autonomy and the Future of Intelligence*

I've always liked the idea of technology being useful in ways that genuinely make people's lives easier. A lot of the projects I've worked on have been around assistive technology. Working on these projects also made me think about something beyond whether the technology actually works. When we build something to help people, are we also helping them become more independent? Or are we slowly making them rely on the system?

There's an interesting paradox here. The more assistance we provide, the less an individual might need to rely on their own capabilities. But reducing assistance isn't necessarily the answer either. Too little support can make tasks inaccessible. The challenge is finding a balance, providing enough assistance to empower someone without taking away their ability to make decisions and act independently. Ultimately, the goal should be to maximise a person's autonomy, not simply the amount of assistance we can provide.

I don't think assistance and independence have to work against each other. But I do think there is a point where we need to be careful about how much we trust the technology we use.

This is one of the reasons I've become interested in AI safety and ethics.

Something that particularly caught my attention while reading about language models was *sycophancy*. It's basically when an AI agrees with you too much, favouring your expressed beliefs or preferences even when they might be wrong. At first, it might sound like a small behavioural issue. But think about what happens when you repeatedly interact with something that always validates your opinions. You might eventually stop questioning it, or even stop questioning yourself. I've been interested in the idea that AI should be supportive without simply telling us what we want to hear. Sometimes, being helpful means disagreeing with someone.

What makes this question even more interesting is that dependence doesn't have to be physical. It can also be intellectual.

I came across two research papers that made me think further about this. [Moral Persuasion in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11731) explores how one language model can attempt to influence another's decisions in morally ambiguous situations. [What Features in Prompts Jailbreak LLMs?](https://aclanthology.org/2025.blackboxnlp-1.28/) looks at why different jailbreak attempts can exploit different mechanisms, and why detecting one type of attack doesn't necessarily mean a model is protected against others.

What I found interesting about these works is that they made me think beyond individual AI responses. What happens when models influence one another? How do we know whether a system will behave safely when the situation is different from the examples it was tested on?

I also started looking more closely at how language models are trained and what exactly we reward them for. We want AI to be helpful, honest and safe, but these are not always easy qualities to measure. A model can learn to produce answers that people prefer without necessarily learning what is actually best for them. And a system that completes a task successfully isn't necessarily one that completes it safely.

I think this becomes much more important as AI systems move from simply answering questions to carrying out tasks, using tools and working with other agents. AI alignment is not just about making systems helpful or harmless; it is also about ensuring that their objectives and behaviour remain consistent with the intentions and values we want them to uphold. Keeping a human involved is not enough by itself. That person should be able to understand what the system is doing, recognise when something is wrong, have the authority to intervene and actually be able to do so.

I know that my work so far is only a starting point. My experience has mostly been around building AI and assistive systems, and I'm still learning about the technical side of AI alignment, control and scalable oversight. But I'm particularly interested in understanding how increasing AI autonomy affects our ability to question, evaluate and supervise these systems. I don't think the answer is to avoid using AI or to make it less capable. There is so much good that these systems can do. I just think we need to pay attention to what we might lose when we start relying on them without questioning them.

Perhaps what we should be asking is not how much more intelligent we can make our machines, but what happens to the parts of ourselves we slowly stop using along the way.

When answers are always a question away, will we still learn to sit with uncertainty? When something can think for us, will we still make the effort to think? I find myself wondering if, in our attempt to build machines that can do everything, we might forget the value of the things only we can choose to do.

Because perhaps the future of intelligence shouldn't be measured only by how much machines can learn, but also by whether we can continue to question, to wonder, to disagree, and to think for ourselves.

*There is a kind of knowing that only comes from not yet knowing, the long pause before the answer, the slow turning of a question in the hand. I hope we never trade that away.*
